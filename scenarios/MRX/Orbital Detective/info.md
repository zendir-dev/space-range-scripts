# Orbital Detective

## Introduction

**Orbital Detective** is a space domain awareness exercise in which each team operates a two-node network: a LEO survey spacecraft and a GEO spacecraft in the equatorial belt. All targets are passive. Nothing broadcasts an identity, and operators cannot command any object they are watching. Classification comes only from the team's own sensors: optical camera, CCD photometry, RADAR, and a laser range finder. Across the session, teams find rogue members in a lookalike constellation, triage tumbling rocket bodies for a commercial capture customer, separate a friendly GEO neighbor from an intruder, image a high-priority object on a transfer toward the GEO belt, and recover their own LEO orbit from GPS.

## Focus Theme

The scenario focuses on custody, photometry, and time-critical cataloging without RF identification. Brightness tells you about a surface, not a name. Range trend tells you who is station-keeping and who is drifting. Spin rate, timed on the laser range finder for nearby bodies or on repeated optical frames for a distant one, tells you whether a tug should attempt capture. The hardest collection is a short LEO horizon window on an object thrown up by an on-orbit event, which must be imaged and matched to a spacecraft identification catalog.

### Who it is for

- Space domain awareness and space-based space surveillance operators who need to track, image, and classify objects using only onboard sensors.
- Mission teams supporting commercial debris-removal tasking who must measure tumble rates and apply capture-safety thresholds.
- Defense, allied, and joint training audiences who need a practical SDA scenario spanning LEO survey, GEO neighborhood watch, and time-critical intercept collection.

## Learning Objectives

- Compare reflectivity across identical-looking satellites with the CCD and identify members whose surface brightness does not match the rest of the group.
- Measure a tumbling body's rotation period with the laser range finder (nearby) or repeated optical imagery (beyond LRF range), convert it to a roll rate, and classify capture safety.
- Use RADAR range trends to tell a stable co-orbital neighbor from a drifting object in the GEO belt.
- Acquire and image a high-priority object inside a short horizon window, then classify it by matching appearance against a reference catalog.
- Confirm a transfer-orbit assessment from ranging and motion, keeping image classification and orbit confirmation as two independent reads.
- Recover semi-major axis, eccentricity, inclination, and altitude of the LEO node from GPS telemetry.
- Split collection across two spacecraft, pointing the rear-facing RADAR and LRF independently of the forward optical and CCD suite.

## Narrative

The Helios constellation leads the LEO node: six satellites that should look alike. Operators hold velocity pointing, survey the cluster, and use CCD photometry to pick out members that are too bright. In parallel, Orbit Recovery Services has contracted a debris triage. Two rocket bodies sit near the GEO node and can be timed on the laser range finder as measured range rises and falls with the tumble. A third body, R/B Delta, is far from both spacecraft. The LRF cannot reach it, so the optical camera has to watch it rotate. Each period becomes a roll rate and a safe, caution, or unsafe capture call.

Two unidentified tracks share the GEO neighborhood. RADAR range over several minutes shows which one holds a bounded station-keeping range and which one drifts. Then a high-priority object from an on-orbit event rises over the LEO horizon in a short window. It is the top collection task: slew, image, match the shape to the catalog, and separately confirm the analysts' transfer-orbit call. Before the session ends, the LEO node's own GPS yields a clean orbit determination.

## Synopsis Teaser

Nothing in the target field will tell you its name. A six-satellite constellation looks uniform until photometry says otherwise. Spent rocket bodies tumble at different rates, and a capture tug needs to know which ones are safe to grab. Two tracks share the GEO belt; one is a neighbor, one is not, and only the range trend will say which. Then an on-orbit event throws a high-priority object toward geostationary altitude, and it will be over the horizon for only a few minutes.

Orbital Detective is space domain awareness as operators actually do it: two nodes, several problems at once, and no friendly transponder to lean on. Teams learn to classify by light, range, spin, and shape, to protect the time-critical shot, and to leave the room with a cataloged picture of a contested orbital neighborhood.
